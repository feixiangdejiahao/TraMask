5   Threats to Validity
The manual labeling of detected bugs as TPs or FPs may introduce subjective bias. To mitigate this
threat, the annotator reviewed all detected bugs twice. A related threat concerns the underlying as-
sumption behind RippleGUItester that observed GUI differences not aligned with the stated change
intent indicate unintended bugs. While this assumption enables RippleGUItester to systematically
detect and report potential bugs, the stated change intent may not fully specify all acceptable
behavioral outcomes of a code change. In particular, some behavioral differences may correspond
to undocumented intended behaviors, beneficial but initially unanticipated changes, or necessary
consequential changes arising from implementing the intended modification. Consequently, some
reported bugs may ultimately be regarded by developers as intended behavior, which may also
influence the reported true positives. Future work will systematically investigate these categories
and develop more comprehensive methods for distinguishing genuine software defects from ac-
ceptable behavioral consequences of code changes. Another threat is the presence of false positives
in the bugs reported by RippleGUItester. To mitigate this threat, we further conducted an in-depth
analysis of the main sources of false positives and discussed concrete improvement directions.
   Our evaluation was conducted on four open-source GUI-based desktop applications, which
may not represent all types of software systems. We mitigate this threat by selecting systems
from different domains (web browser, document editor, reference manager, and game engine)
with large and actively maintained codebases. Furthermore, RippleGUItester is not tailored to any
specific application or framework, suggesting that the approach can be adapted to other GUI-based
systems. Another threat concerns the choice of LLMs and computational resources. We used state-
of-the-art models to explore the upper bound of achievable performance, and results may differ
when using smaller or less capable models. However, this design choice allows us to evaluate the
feasibility and potential of change-aware GUI testing without conflating methodological limitations
with model constraints. Future work can explore cost–performance trade-offs with alternative
models. Execution time could also be further reduced through engineering optimizations, such as
parallelizing the execution of generated tests.

6   Related Work
Exploratory Testing. Exploratory testing has been shown to be effective at the system testing
level [1, 3, 4, 9, 16, 19, 23, 36, 39]. A variety of principles and guidelines for conducting and
managing exploratory testing have been proposed [3, 7, 8, 23, 38]. Several tools and techniques have
been developed to support exploratory testing. Tapir [6] generates navigational test cases to reduce
duplication. Scenario-based exploratory testing, also known as soap opera testing [7], designs
complex and realistic usage scenarios to provoke failures that are difficult to anticipate upfront.
Recent work [30–32] supports soap opera testing by constructing a system knowledge graph that
captures user tasks and failures, and by generating test scenarios through combinations of related
behaviors. SoapOperaTG [31] is a tool for generating and presenting such test scenarios. Su et
al. [34] automate the execution of these exploratory test scenarios and associated bug detection with
human-like creativity and intelligence. Despite their strong capabilities in test scenario generation,
execution, and bug detection, these approaches are not code-change aware, as they do not leverage
information about the specific code changes being introduced. In contrast, our work is explicitly
triggered by a code change and systematically explores potentially impacted scenarios to uncover
bugs introduced by that change.
